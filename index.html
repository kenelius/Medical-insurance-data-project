• This project demonstrates how data science is used in healthcare to analyze medical insurance data and understand the factors that influence healthcare costs. The analysis focuses on identifying relationships between demographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, and insurance charges to generate meaningful insights for healthcare decision-making and predictive modeling.

Project Objectives¶

The project seeks to:

  • Clean and preprocess healthcare insurance data for analysis.
  • Explore patterns and trends in medical insurance charges.
  • Investigate how demographic factors such as age and gender influence healthcare expenses.
  • Examine the impact of lifestyle factors such as smoking habits and Body Mass Index (BMI) on insurance costs.
  • Analyze family-related variables such as the number of dependents covered by insurance.
  • Compare insurance charges across different geographic regions.

Key insight:¶

Insurance charges are heavily skewed to the right, meaning most policyholders incur relatively low medical costs while only a few experience very high expenses. The highest concentration of charges falls around the lower cost range, roughly between $2,000 and $4,000. However, the distribution stretches far to the right, with some individuals recording costs as high as about $19,000–$20,000. This suggests the presence of a smaller high-cost group within the dataset.

Such a pattern is common in healthcare expenditure data because most people only require routine or moderate medical care, whereas a minority face expensive treatments due to chronic illnesses, severe medical conditions, or risky lifestyle behaviors. From a statistical and modeling perspective, this strong skewness can affect prediction accuracy if the raw data is used directly. Therefore, techniques such as log transformation or robust regression methods may be necessary to reduce the influence of extreme values and produce more reliable insurance cost predictions.

Conclusion.¶

  • Model Explanatory Power (R-squared):

    • The R-squared is 0.295, meaning that age, BMI, and the number of children account for only 29.5% of the variance in medical charges. Over 70% of the variation is driven by factors not included in this specific model.

  • Impact of Individual Variables (Coefficients):

Age (209.53): There is a clear positive relationship. For every additional year of age, medical charges increase by approximately $209.53, assuming all else is held constant.

Children (415.13): Having dependents also increases costs. For every additional child, charges rise by roughly $415.13.

BMI (-127.89): Interestingly, this model suggests a negative relationship, where each 1-unit increase in BMI decreases charges by about $127.89. (Note: In real-world medical data, BMI usually increases charges. This negative coefficient strongly suggests "omitted variable bias"—likely because a major driver like "smoking" was left out, skewing the math for the variables that remain).

  • Statistical Significance (P>|t|):

The p-values for all predictors (age, bmi, children) are 0.000. This means the relationships observed are highly statistically significant, and we can be confident these results are not just due to random chance in this dataset.
